## **1. The Story in Brief**

Every year on Makar Sankranti, the rooftops of Jaipur fill with people and the sky fills with paper kites. For three years, Ananya Sharma has had one job on her family’s roof: holding the charkhi, the wooden spool, while her older brother Rohan flies. He wins the duels. She’s the “good assistant.”

This year, Rohan comes home from a secret errand with eight stitches in his hand and four lies. A poster in the lane announces a duel on January 14 between Rohan and the flashy Vikram Mehta. Rohan can’t fly with his right hand. Papa says no. And Ananya, who has never once flown a duel, pins the copy of the poster she drew on her bedroom wall and writes her own name under Rohan’s, in pencil.

To build a kite worth flying, she needs a color worth seeing. So she takes something that isn’t hers: a square of saffron cotton from her father’s workshop, marked in his notebook “Redo 43. Do not cut.” That stolen flame becomes the heart of her kite, and the beginning of a long chain of lies, repairs, and hard choices on a roof where a nearly invisible line can cut far more than paper.

## **2. Where the Story Happens**

### **India and Rajasthan**

India is a country in South Asia and, with about 1.4 billion people, the most populous country in the world. It has hundreds of languages. Hindi and English are used by the national government, and each state has its own languages too. Ananya’s family speaks Hindi, and a few Hindi words run through her notebook.

Rajasthan, in the northwest, is India’s largest state by area. Much of it is covered by the Thar Desert. It is famous for its forts and palaces, its bright textiles, its music and dance, and its food. Its capital is Jaipur.

### **Places in the Book**

* **The lane and the haveli.** The Sharmas live in an old house built around a courtyard. Papa calls it “the haveli,” even though one wing is closed off, two windows don’t shut, and the roof leaks in three places. Pigeons own the north ledge, and the orange cat owns everything else.

* **Johari Bazaar**, one of the old city’s great market streets. Mrs. Arora’s boutique is nearby.

* **Chandpole**, a gate and neighborhood of the old city, where Rohan’s hand is stitched up.

* **Sanganer**, a textile center near Jaipur famous for hand block printing on cotton.

* **Jal Mahal**, where fireworks light up the sky on festival night.

## **3. Makar Sankranti: A Sky Full of Kites**

### **The Festival**

Makar Sankranti marks the day the sun enters the sign of Makara, or Capricorn, in the Indian calendar. Because the festival follows the sun instead of the moon, its date barely moves: it falls on January 14, or sometimes January 15. This season of solar and harvest festivals takes different forms across regions, including Pongal in Tamil Nadu and Uttarayan in Gujarat.

In Jaipur, Sankranti means kites. From early morning, whole families climb onto their flat roofs with tea, snacks, music, and piles of paper kites. People visit, pray, and give to those in need. Sesame and jaggery sweets such as til ke laddu and gajak are everywhere. At night the sky fills with lit paper lanterns called kandeel, and with fireworks.

### **How a Kite Duel Works**

A **patang** is a light fighting kite shaped like a diamond, made of thin paper stretched over two bamboo sticks. It has no tail and can be steered with tiny movements of the string. The string is wound on a **charkhi**, a wooden spool held by a second person. That’s Ananya’s job at the start of the book.

In a duel, two flyers cross their lines in the sky. Each tries to cut the other’s string. The flyer can **dheel**, letting the string run out fast, or **kheench**, pulling it in with quick tugs. When one kite is cut loose and drifts away, everyone shouts ***Woh kaata!***, “It’s cut!”

The spool holder matters more than people think. A good one keeps the string from tangling, feeds it out smoothly, and knows when to stop. In Chapter 15, the Sharmas choose two emergency words for Ananya to shout: **FULL DHEEL**. They mean that Rohan must let the whole line run out, even if the kite is lost.

## **4. Daily Life in the Lane**

### **Family and Home**

The Sharma family lives the way many families in Jaipur’s old city do: close together, with neighbors on every side and a roof that works as a terrace, a drying line, a playground, and on Sankranti, a stadium. Children call adults outside the family Aunty and Uncle as a sign of respect, as in Gopal Uncle and Farida Aunty.

### **Food**

Ananya’s notebook is full of food. Breakfast might be **poha**, spiced flattened rice, or a **paratha**, a flaky flatbread that’s often stuffed. Every day there is **chapati**, a round flat bread. Rajasthan’s special dish is **dal baati**, baked dough balls served with lentils. On festival day, the roofs smell of **masala chai**, spiced milky tea, hot **pakode**, vegetable fritters, and sweets: **til ke laddu**, **gajak**, and **feeni**.

### **Faith and Festivals**

Jaipur is a city of many faiths. Ananya hears temple bells and the call to prayer in the same morning. At home, Ma lights the **puja** lamp, a small daily prayer and offering. The book doesn’t dwell on religion, but it’s part of the rhythm of every day in the lane.

### **Dance**

Ma studied **kathak**, a classical dance of north India, for six years. Its name comes from the word for “story,” and kathak dancers tell stories with quick, rhythmic footwork, spins, and bells worn at the ankles. In Chapter 9, Ma teaches Ananya something she never expected a dance to teach her: how to “listen with the floor.”

### **Money**

India’s money is the rupee (₹). On January 14, 2025, the reference exchange rates worked out to about 86.57 rupees per U.S. dollar: Ananya’s twenty rupees were about 23 U.S. cents. It is everything she has, and Papa tells her to keep it. This dated comparison does not measure local purchasing power.

## **6. The Line That Cuts: Kite String, Birds, and Safety**

### **Why String Can Be Dangerous**

To win duels, flyers want string that cuts. Traditional **manjha** is cotton thread coated with glue and finely ground glass. It can cut skin as well as other kites’ strings, which is why experienced flyers protect their fingers.

**Nylon and other synthetic kite lines** can cause severe injuries and persist in the environment. The name “Chinese manjha” does not establish where a line was made: some is manufactured in India. Cotton can also injure or entangle birds, especially when coated with an abrasive. No discarded line is harmless.

In July 2017, India’s National Green Tribunal, a special court for environmental cases, banned the making, sale, storage, purchase, and use of kite string made of nylon or other synthetic materials across the whole country. In the book, that banned line is the “blue filament” that runs through the whole book, from Rohan’s injury in Chapter 2 to the duel in Chapter 17. As Ananya realizes in Chapter 2, “You don’t need crushed glass to cut.”

### **Birds in the Sky**

The black kite is a bird of prey that soars over Indian cities. Rescue teams in Jaipur treat pigeons, black kites, and other birds injured by kite string. Lines can also injure people on foot, on bicycles, or on motorcycles and scooters.

During the festival, volunteers and veterinarians in Jaipur run rescue hotlines and bird care centers. Local authorities ask people not to fly kites early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when the most birds are in the air.

### **The Safety Rules in the Book**

The book’s Note gives the most important rule: **never pull or pick up found string with bare hands. Tell an adult, and leave a caught bird to trained rescuers.** In Chapter 10, a volunteer tells the children they did the right thing by calling for help (Meena made the call) instead of trying to pull the string off.

In Chapter 15, Ananya, Rohan, Meena, and their parents write nine **Sharma Roof Rules** and tape them to the door of the roof:

1. Plain cotton only.

2. An adult stays with us from launch until landing.

3. Nobody goes closer than two big steps to the edge wall.

4. Closed shoes. Water. Bandages.

5. Nobody chases a kite into the street.

6. If a strange line touches our roof, nobody grabs it.

7. When someone says stop, everyone stops, even if the duel is almost won.

8. The charkhi stays in the low crate when it spins fast.

9. The number for the bird rescue line is posted by the stairs.

**Fiction and practice.** The nine rules above belong to the family in the novel. “Two big steps” is not a fall-protection standard. For a real activity, choose a clear space at ground level with an adult and check local guidance. Plain cotton can still tangle and cause injuries. “Full dheel,” the duels, and the rescue in Chapter 17 are not exercises to copy. Do not handle a bird caught in string: have an adult contact trained rescuers. The lit lanterns in the story are not a suggested activity either.

## **7. The Color of Things: Dyeing in Jaipur**

### **A City of Printed Cloth**

For centuries, the Jaipur region has been famous for textiles. In the towns of Sanganer and Bagru, artisans print cotton by hand with carved wooden blocks, pressing color onto the cloth one block at a time. Before and after printing, the cloth goes through many other hands: people who wash it, dye it, fix the color, dry it in the sun, and finish it.

Papa’s work sits in the middle of that chain. He isn’t “a master dyer from a family that goes back centuries,” as Ananya once told Meena to impress her. He adjusts dye baths, rescues uneven batches, and finishes fabric for printers, shops, and tailors. He tests every color on numbered sample squares and records everything in his black notebook. Square 43 is a sample of **kesariya**, a deep saffron color.

### **Where Colors Come From**

Today most fabric is colored with synthetic dyes, but many Jaipur artisans still use natural ones:

| Color | Natural source |
| :---- | :---- |
| Blue | **Indigo**, from the leaves of the indigo plant |
| Red and pink | **Indian madder** (*manjistha*), from the roots of *Rubia cordifolia* |
| Yellow | **Turmeric** rhizome, which gives a bright yellow that fades in the sun, and pomegranate rind |
| Black and gray | Rusty iron soaked with jaggery, which reacts with plant tannins |

Some natural dyes need a **mordant**, a substance such as alum that helps the color bind to the fiber. That’s one reason color is so hard to control: the same dye can come out differently depending on the cloth, the water, the heat, and the time.

**Is kesariya made with saffron?** Not in Papa’s workshop. *Kesariya* is the name of a color, the deep orange of saffron. The real spice, which Ma keeps for holidays, would dye cloth yellow, with durability depending on the process and exposure to light.

### **What Papa Teaches About Color**

Papa has a way of talking about color that is really about people:

* “Color never lives alone. It changes with the light, the fabric, and whatever you put next to it.”

* When Ananya asks if that means no color is ever true: “They’re all true in their own conditions.”

* After the theft: “A color taken in secret can’t prove you deserve to be seen.”

## **8. Discussion Questions**

### **Understanding the Story**

**Before I Begin and Part One: The Ones Who Hold the Spool (Chapters 1 to 4)**

1. Mrs. Patel tells the class, “Personal doesn’t mean perfect. Personal means honest.” Ananya compares honesty to the door of Papa’s workshop. What does that door have in common with the truth?

2. Rohan calls Ananya a “good assistant.” How does she feel about holding the spool?

3. “Rohan came home with eight stitches and four lies.” What really happened to his hand? Why does Ananya agree to keep his secret?

4. What is written on the poster in the lane? What does Ananya add in pencil to her own copy?

5. What is square “KESARIYA 43,” and what did Papa write about it? Why does Ananya take it anyway?

**Part Two: Agni (Chapters 5 to 9)**

6. How does the kite get its name?

7. Why does Rohan write “NEVER WRAP THE STRING AROUND YOUR FINGERS” in capital letters? Whose idea was it?

8. Meena agrees to help “with safety and measurements,” but not with hiding the theft. Is that a fair line to draw? Does she always keep to it, especially in Chapter 8?

9. Farida Aunty looks at the first Agni and says, “Pretty color. Bad kite.” What goes wrong, and what do they change?

10. What does Ananya discover about Ma in Chapter 9? What does “listen with the floor” mean?

**Part Three: The Line That Cuts (Chapters 10 to 14)**

11. A black kite falls into the courtyard. What do the children do right?

12. Ma tells Rohan, “You don’t turn your sister into a safe when what you’re locking away can hurt someone.” What does she mean?

13. How is the missing square discovered? Ananya says, “I’m sorry.” Papa answers, “You got caught.” What’s the difference?

14. How does Ananya begin to repair what she broke? What does Papa give her instead of the stolen square?

15. Why does Ananya apologize to Meena without asking her to be friends again?

**Part Four and Epilogue: The Sky Keeps No Score (Chapters 15 to 18)**

16. Which of the Sharma Roof Rules turns out to matter most on festival day?

17. How does Ananya win her first duel? How does it feel?

18. In Chapter 17, Ananya counts her bad decisions one by one. What are they? What happens when Ananya shouts “Full dheel!”?

19. After the duel, Meena asks for the result to write in her log. Ananya first says “None,” then “Write *stopped*.” Why isn’t it the same thing?

20. Why does Ananya tell the truth in front of a camera? What does Vikram admit?

21. In the Epilogue, Ananya ties a paper tag marked “A. S.” to her new charkhi. What does it mean next to Rohan’s carved “R.S.”?

### **Thinking Deeper**

1. The book is called *The Stolen Flame*. What exactly was stolen, and from whom? Can a win built on a lie prove anything?

2. Both siblings break rules. Rohan tries to buy banned string; Ananya takes the square and lies. Is one wrong worse than the other? Why?

3. Meena tells Ananya, “Sometimes you talk about your roof like it’s the whole city.” Later, Ananya sees girls and women flying all around her. Was the thing holding her back the city, her family, or something else?

4. Papa tells Ananya, “Next time, ask me before you tell something that’s mine.” Mrs. Patel promises to read only the pages marked with a star. When is a story yours to tell, and when does it belong to someone else?

5. Rohan and Papa both say, “I can do both.” What do they each mean?

6. The Sharma rule says, “When someone says stop, everyone stops, even if the duel is almost won.” Why is that the hardest rule to keep?

7. Part One is called “The Ones Who Hold the Spool.” Who are the people in the book whose work nobody sees? Who are they in your own life?

8. Near the very end, a kesariya sample starts to slide off the table. Ananya writes, “The first time, I would have taken it,” and calls Papa instead: “Come look. The color changes in the wind.” What has changed in her?

## **9. Activities**

**1. Notebook endings.** Ananya ends many chapters with little headings: “Color of the Day,” “What I Didn’t Say Today,” “Wind Report.” Write a notebook page about a real or invented day, keeping anything you wish private and end it with three headings of your own invention.

**2. Design Agni.** Draw a patang to scale on paper: a diamond with a spine and a bow. Decide on your colors and explain why. If you fly a kite for real, use plain cotton string only, in an open field far from roads and power lines, with an adult.

**3. Kite geometry.** Draw a diamond model with diagonals that cross at right angles. Its area is the product of the diagonals divided by two. What is the area when the diagonals are 12 and 16 cm long? If you double both lengths, how many times larger is the area? This geometric model simplifies the shape of a real patang.

**4. Color lab.** An adult prepares separate infusions of turmeric, onion skins, and red cabbage, then lets them cool completely. Without adding alum or any other product, color three small squares of the same white cotton, one in each infusion, for the same length of time. Protect the table, wear gloves, and do not taste anything; keep the experiment’s utensils out of food preparation. Once the squares are dry, cover half of each with opaque card. Place all three together near a window for a week. Remove the cards and compare the two halves of each square. Record it if nothing changes: this observation does not measure the long-term durability of a professional dye.

**5. Color never lives alone.** Cut two small squares of the same orange paper. Put one on a black background and one on a white background. Do they look the same? Write two sentences about a person who seems different depending on who is next to them.

**6. Roof rules.** Choose an activity you love, such as a sport, cooking, or biking, and write your own list of rules to keep everyone safe. Your list must include a “stop” rule, like the Sharmas’ rule 7.

**7. The true version.** In Chapter 18, rumors about the duel grow into a heroic story. Write the rumor version of a scene from the book in five sentences. Then write the true version. Which is more interesting? Which is more useful?

**8. Invisible hands.** Pick an object you use at school, such as your shirt, a notebook, or a pencil. List every person whose hands it passed through before it reached you: grower, spinner, dyer, printer, driver, seller. Whose names do we never learn?

**9. A festival safety campaign.** Design a poster or short video for a kite festival. It should teach people to fly with cotton only, avoid the hours when birds fly most, never grab found string, and call the rescue line for a hurt bird.

**10. Debate.** “The duel in Chapter 17 should have counted as a win for Ananya.” Argue both sides, using what happened on the roof and the Sharma Roof Rules.

## **11. For Teachers and Parents**

**Reading pace.** The four parts make natural stopping points, and the chapters are short. One part a week, with the questions for that part, works well. Read the opening page with Part One and the Epilogue with Part Four.

**What to be aware of.** The book includes a hand injury from kite string (Rohan’s stitches), two birds hurt by synthetic string, a theft and a series of lies, and a public confession. Nothing is graphic. The injured birds survive in this fiction, but that outcome is never guaranteed in real life. The children call for help in Chapter 10; they also take risks elsewhere in the story. Help readers distinguish what they do, what they should have done, and what an adult must take responsibility for.

**Safety first.** If your class flies kites, use plain cotton string only, never coated or synthetic string, and choose an open space far from roads and power lines. Make sure no one ever wraps string around their fingers or picks up found string with bare hands.

**Framing gender carefully.** It’s tempting to read the book as the story of a girl held back by her culture. The book is more careful than that. Women argue about kite sticks in the market, the Bansal twins win their duel, and Ma turns out to know how to fly. Family habits and the role she is allowed to play limit Ananya; her difficulty asking for help matters too, without making her responsible for unfair restrictions. Invite students to notice the evidence before they draw conclusions about India or about any culture.

**Faith and diversity.** Jaipur is home to people of several faiths, and the book reflects that lightly: a puja lamp, a temple bell, the call to prayer. There’s no need to label characters’ religions beyond what the book says.

**Repairing and learning.** Ananya’s progress does not erase the harm caused by the theft. She returns the remaining cloth, helps put things right, and learns to give up winning when danger appears. In the Epilogue, her work to make amends comes to an end; returning to the workshop becomes a choice to learn. Does success undo a wrong? Ground the discussion in the characters’ actions.

**Questions across the books**

1. Ananya and Lamine both lie for the sake of a dream, and someone else pays part of the price. Compare the order Papa nearly loses with Mrs. Arora and the customer from Ziguinchor that Lamine’s mother loses.

2. Ananya’s teacher promises to read only the pages marked with a star. Lamine’s father reads his journal without asking. Katya decides what can leave her notebook. Who should decide who reads your writing?

3. Rohan tries to run Ananya’s flying as if her hands were his. Compare his attitude with Sokha’s when he tries to help Maly, then with Awa’s toward Lamine. When does a sibling help, and when does a sibling take over?
