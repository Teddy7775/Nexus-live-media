## **1. The Story in Brief**

Sokha is twelve, quick with a plan, and not always quick to listen. His sister Maly is nine. She lost her right leg at four, on a path that had never been officially checked for mines, and she walks with a wooden crutch tied with a pink ribbon. Their father died in a road accident when Sokha was eight. Their mother, Mae, works in other people’s fields, sorts rice, and sells vegetables at the market.

The village school is a borrowed room upstairs in Lok Ta Ming’s house, run three days a week by Neak Kru Lina. Then two things happen at once. The family that owns the house is coming back and needs its room. A farm path is closed after something is found near the ditch. A few days later, an explosion badly hurts a neighbor, and the closure is extended to the path to school. Now the road is longer, the class will soon have to move, and Sokha is sure he knows exactly how to fix everything for Maly.

He is wrong about that, and the book is honest about why. *The Road to the Bell* is a story about a brother who learns to ask before he helps, a sister who insists on telling her own story, and a village that decides school is worth carrying from one house to another.

## **2. Where the Story Happens**

### **Cambodia**

Cambodia is a country in Southeast Asia, between Thailand, Laos, and Vietnam, with a short coastline on the Gulf of Thailand. The 2019 census counted about 15.6 million people. The capital is Phnom Penh. The Mekong River and the great lake of Tonle Sap shape the land, the fishing, and the rice harvests.

The year has two main seasons. The dry season runs from about November to April, and April is the hottest month. The rainy season usually begins in May. That’s why the storm in Chapter 15 feels like a change of season: rain is plausible at this time of year, though its arrival varies.

### **Banteay Meanchey Province**

Sokha’s village is invented, but its province is real. Banteay Meanchey sits in the northwest corner of Cambodia, on the border with Thailand. About 860,000 people lived there at the 2019 census. Its capital is Serei Saophoan. Most families farm rice, raise animals, and trade at local markets. Many men and women also cross into Thailand for seasonal work.

The northwest border provinces, Banteay Meanchey among them, hold much of the land in Cambodia that still has to be cleared of mines. In the 1980s, armies laid a long belt of mines along the Thai border, and some of that land has never been checked.

### **Battambang**

Battambang, a large city just south of the province, appears in the book as the place Sokha fears Maly may have to travel to. It is home to a regional physical rehabilitation center, where people who have lost a limb or have trouble walking can be fitted with artificial limbs, braces, crutches, and wheelchairs. The center is run by Cambodia’s Ministry of Social Affairs with support from the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), and its services are free. As Neak Kru Lina warns Sokha, though, “Free doesn’t mean easy.” The ICRC describes this arrangement in its 2022 account; its 2024 report confirms continued support for the centers. The novel’s May 3 visit and its staff are fictional: this guide does not offer an appointment or a guarantee of individual care.

### **The Village of Chhouk Ksant**

Chhouk Ksant looks like many villages in the region. Wooden houses stand on stilts, with tools, animals, and shade underneath. There is a wat, a market in the nearest town, rice fields all around, and paths that everyone knows by heart. Some land beyond those paths has never been checked. In the story, “the ground everybody knows” means the places where the children are used to staying. Familiarity does not certify that land is safe: a well-used path can still be dangerous, as Maly’s accident shows.

## **3. Cambodia’s Past and the Land Today**

### **A Short Timeline**

| When | What happened |
| :---- | :---- |
| 800s to 1400s | The Khmer Empire rules much of mainland Southeast Asia. Angkor Wat is built in the 1100s. |
| 1863 to 1953 | Cambodia is a French protectorate. It becomes independent on November 9, 1953. |
| 1970 to 1975 | Civil war. The country is also heavily bombed during the war in neighboring Vietnam. |
| April 17, 1975, to January 7, 1979 | The Khmer Rouge regime. Cities are emptied, schools and temples are closed, and between 1.5 and 2 million people die of execution, hunger, and disease. |
| 1979 to 1991 | Vietnamese forces drive out the Khmer Rouge, but fighting goes on for years. All sides lay mines, especially along the Thai border. |
| 1991 to 1993 | The Paris Peace Agreements, a United Nations mission, and elections. The monarchy is restored in 1993. |
| 1992 | The Cambodian Mine Action Centre (CMAC) is founded to clear mines. |
| 1997 | Cambodia signs the international treaty that bans antipersonnel mines. It ratifies the treaty in 1999. |
| 2025 | Treaty deadline for clearing antipersonnel mine areas. In December 2025, an extension is granted until December 31, 2030. |

Many Cambodian families still carry memories of the Khmer Rouge years. The book never mentions that period, and younger readers don’t need its details to understand Sokha’s story. What matters here is what those decades of war left in the ground.

### **What War Left in the Ground**

Landmines and other explosive remnants of war don’t stop working when a war ends. They stay hidden in fields, forests, and along paths for decades. Between January 1979 and December 2025, Cambodia’s CMVIS recorded more than 65,000 people killed or injured, including more than 9,000 people who lost a limb.

The good news is real too. Casualties have fallen sharply as land has been cleared. In 2024, 49 casualties were recorded, including 16 children under 18. The December 2025 CMVIS report gives a provisional total of 39 for 2025. These totals include people killed and injured; they do not refer only to injured civilians. Each of those numbers is still a person, a family, and a road that changed.

### **After the Story Ends**

In July 2025, and again in December 2025, armed clashes broke out along the border between Cambodia and Thailand, including in parts of Banteay Meanchey. Hundreds of thousands of people on both sides had to leave their homes for a time, and new mine blasts near the border became part of the dispute. A ceasefire was signed at the end of December 2025. If students ask, keep to the facts and avoid taking sides. The book itself takes place before these events.

## **4. Daily Life in the Village**

### **Home and Work**

Sokha’s family lives in a house on stilts, like most of their neighbors. The space underneath is where people rest in the heat, keep tools, and shelter animals, including Mae’s red hen, who pecks a corner off the first page of Sokha’s diary. Mae earns money in pieces: field labor for a landowner, sorting rice, and selling vegetables at the market. Every riel counts, as Sokha’s Market Money list shows in Chapter 5.

### **Money**

Cambodia’s currency is the riel. U.S. dollars are also used every day, often in the same shop, and this guide uses a rounded rate of 4,000 riels to 1 U.S. dollar (USD), not Canadian dollar, for its calculations. The actual rate varies. When Sokha writes that Mae sold 48,000 riels of vegetables, that’s about twelve U.S. dollars in sales before expenses, not net profit.

### **Food**

Two dishes appear in the book. **Num banh chok** is rice noodles with a fragrant herb sauce, often made with fish. **Kuy teav** is a rice noodle soup. Rice is at the heart of nearly every meal, along with fish, vegetables, and herbs. The family’s market list includes rice, dried fish, and cooking oil.

### **Belief**

Buddhism, mainly Theravada, is the religion of a large majority of Cambodians. The wat, a Buddhist temple and monastery, is the center of village life: a place for prayer, festivals, advice, and sometimes classes. Many families also honor the **Neak Ta**, guardian spirits of a place. In Chapter 1, Mae leaves water and rice at a small shrine under a tree and whispers, “Protect my children’s roads.”

### **Respect in Words**

Cambodians use words of respect that show age and relationship. A younger sibling calls an older one *Bong*. Children call an older man *Lok Ta* and an older woman *Lok Yeay*. Children can address a female teacher as *Neak Kru*. Sokha uses these words the way any Cambodian child would, without thinking about them.

### **Khmer New Year**

**Chaul Chnam Thmey** is Cambodia’s biggest holiday. It falls in the middle of April, at the end of the harvest and the hottest part of the dry season, and lasts three days. In 2025 the official holiday ran from Monday, April 14, to Wednesday, April 16. Sokha starts his diary the day before, and Chapters 1 to 3 fall on those three days. Families visit the wat, bring food to the monks, clean and decorate their homes, and play traditional games. Splashing water and powder is part of the fun, and so is tug of war.

### **School**

Cambodian children go to primary school for six years, from about age six, and then to lower and upper secondary school. Most children now finish primary school, but many rural families still face long distances, costs, and the need for children to help at home. Lina’s community class is fictional. Its three mornings a week and its April 16 session on a public holiday do not represent the regular Cambodian school calendar.

## **6. Landmines, Safety, and the Road to School**

### **The One Rule That Matters Most**

The book gives three reminders: **do not approach, touch nothing, alert an adult.** Never enter a closed area or place your own marker near a suspicious object. If you think you are already in a minefield, stop: do not try to get out by yourself or retrace your steps. Call for help without inviting anyone to come toward you. Adults should contact specialist services. Trained educators should explain the local safety guidance.

This rule isn’t only for Cambodia. Old explosives still turn up in farm fields and construction sites in Europe, the Pacific, and elsewhere, decades after wars ended. Safety messages must take account of local conditions and hazards.

### **How Land Is Made Safe**

1. **Survey.** Teams talk with villagers about accidents and dangerous places, then mark suspected areas, often with red and white rope or red warning signs. That’s the rope that closes the path in Chapter 4.

2. **Search.** Deminers work slowly, inch by inch, with metal detectors, trained dogs, and even trained rats.

3. **Destroy.** Mines that are found are destroyed where they lie, or moved by specialists.

4. **Release.** Land release relies on surveys, clearance where needed, and checks required by national standards. The responsible authorities confirm the boundaries and conditions for use.

Mr. Kosal puts it simply in Chapter 12: “A map doesn’t prove anything is safe.” A resident’s visual check is not enough: follow current guidance from the responsible authorities. That’s why, when a tired adult suggests the old path on moving day, Sokha and Uncle Sarath say no.

### **A Hero with Whiskers**

For five years, a giant African pouched rat named **Magawa** sniffed out landmines in Cambodia for the Belgian organization APOPO. APOPO uses their sense of smell in work supervised by specialists. Their low weight helps when detecting pressure-activated mines; this does not mean any animal can be sent into a suspected minefield. Magawa found more than 100 landmines and other explosives. In 2020 he became the first rat to receive a British gold medal for animal bravery. He retired in 2021, and died in 2022. A statue of him was unveiled in Siem Reap on April 3, 2026, after the period covered by the novel.

### **Getting Around with a Disability**

Maly’s story is about a leg, but it’s even more about a road. In Chapter 3, the whole class moves forward “one hand’s length” so Maly has room. In Chapter 11 she tells the village meeting, “If you’re making a plan for kids, ask the kids how they walk.” That’s the heart of what disability rights groups mean when they say “nothing about us without us.”

Mobility aids such as crutches, braces, and artificial limbs work best when they are chosen and adjusted with the person who uses them and with trained professionals. A child grows, so a crutch or a prosthetic limb may need adjustments or replacement as the child grows. It’s also why a secret fix is risky: the cover Sokha makes in Chapter 9, without asking Maly or anyone trained, slips in Chapter 10. And it’s why the assessment day in Chapter 13 matters: the specialist finds that Maly’s old crutch is too tall and its tip is worn out.

## **7. Discussion Questions**

### **Understanding the Story**

**Prologue and Part One: Known Roads (Chapters 1 to 4)**

1. In the Prologue, Maly has to stay home on New Year’s Day, and Sokha promises that next year he’ll find a way to take her to the wat. What does Maly answer? Look for the same idea again in the Epilogue. What has changed?

2. What are Vannak’s five soccer rules? Why does the fifth rule suddenly matter so much in Chapter 2?

3. In Chapter 3, Neak Kru Lina says that as long as they can hear the little bicycle bell, school exists somewhere. What does she mean?

4. What does Mr. Kosal say about the ball in Chapter 4? Do you agree?

**Part Two: The Detour (Chapters 5 to 8)**

5. Maly’s notebook is lying open in Chapter 5. What interrupts him? How does he later explain himself to Maly? Does his answer seem completely honest?

6. After the explosion near the ditch, Maly tells Sokha to “Ask better.” What do you think she wants from him?

7. Why does Sokha hide the brochure in Chapter 7? Look at his list, “What Something Free Might Cost.” Which item surprised you most?

8. At the fork in the road in Chapter 8, Sokha chooses the long way. What makes that choice hard?

**Part Three: What I Didn’t Ask (Chapters 9 to 12)**

9. What is Sokha’s secret plan, and what does he tell other people to get the materials?

10. What happens when Maly takes her fourth step in the yard in Chapter 10? Why does Maly untie her ribbon?

11. At the village meeting, Sokha gives Maly his blue pen instead of reading his speech. Why is that the right choice?

12. Aunt Sophea says, “You can have good hands and still make a bad choice with them.” What does she mean?

**Part Four and Epilogue: The Sound That Carries (Chapters 13 to 16)**

13. What does Ms. Sreyneang find out about Maly’s old crutch on assessment day?

14. On moving day, a man suggests pushing the cart down the old path. What does Sokha say, and what does Uncle Sarath add?

15. After the storm, Sokha asks Maly, “What do you think we should do?” What changes after that question?

16. Why does the class wait for Maly in Chapter 16? What does the bell sound like when she rings it?

17. In the Epilogue, what does each of them write on the page they share?

### **Thinking Deeper**

1. Sokha loves his sister and wants to protect her. So what exactly goes wrong? Is it possible to help someone in a way that hurts them?

2. Maly says, “You fixed what you could see. You never asked me what I wanted.” Have you ever helped someone without asking first? How did it turn out?

3. Maly crosses out a sentence in her story, “Her brother built something wonderful and carried her all the way to school,” and writes, “The girl asked for what she needed.” Why does she change it? Whose story is it?

4. Sokha promised not to read Maly’s notebook. Why does privacy matter so much between siblings? What would have changed if he had read it?

5. The group of slower walkers is renamed “with breaks.” Why do names like that matter?

6. The book shows a whole village keeping a school alive. Who helps, and how? Which kind of help do you think mattered most?

7. The book ends with a promise from Sokha: “Next time, I’ll ask first. (Even if my idea is brilliant. Especially if my idea is brilliant.)” Why “especially”?

8. The title is *The Road to the Bell*. Is the book really about a road? About a bell? About something else?

## **8. Activities**

**1. A diary in lists.** Sokha often thinks in lists: “What the Ball Cost Me” and “What I Got to Keep,” “Market Money,” “What Something Free Might Cost.” Write a diary page about a real day in your life using at least one list. Try a pair of lists that pull against each other, the way Sokha’s do.

**2. Your Book of Steps.** In Chapter 12, the village draws a map of the safe road with its breaks: Lok Yeay Chan’s awning and Aunt Dara’s mango tree. Draw a map of your own route to school. Mark the places where someone who walks slowly, uses a wheelchair, or pushes a stroller could rest, and the places that would be hard for them. Work from memory or with an adult, without exploring unfamiliar paths. Ask someone who uses the route what barriers or needs you missed, without speaking for them.

**3. Ask first.** Choose a scene where Sokha acts without asking. Rewrite it so that he asks Maly three good questions before he does anything. How does the scene change? Act out both versions with a partner.

**4. The village meeting.** Recreate the meeting under the tree from Chapter 11. Assign roles: the village chief, Lok Ta Phirun, Uncle Phearak, Mae, Neak Kru Lina, Uncle Sarath, Vannak, Sokha, and Maly. The question: should the school go on, where, and how will the children get there safely? Everyone must speak, and the meeting must end with a plan.

**5. Market math.** Use Sokha’s list from Chapter 5. Mae receives 48,000 riels for her vegetables. She spends 31,000 on rice, dried fish, oil, and gas, then sets aside 4,000. Check that 13,000 riels are left over. Using the exercise’s rounded rate of 4,000 riels to 1 U.S. dollar, how much is each item in dollars? What would your family’s market list look like?

**6. A page in two voices.** Sokha and Maly end the book by writing on the same page. With a partner, write about something you did together, each of you in your own voice, on one sheet of paper. Don’t correct each other’s part.

**7. A safety poster.** Make a poster that teaches the book’s rule: stay far away, touch nothing, tell an adult. Use simple symbols. Before sharing it, an adult should check the message against official guidance for the area. Never use real or suspicious objects as models.

**8. The sound of school.** For Neak Kru Lina, school exists wherever you can hear the bell. What sound tells you school has started? Record it, draw it, or describe it in five lines.

**9. Debate.** “A school should only open in a real school building.” Split the class into two sides. Use examples from the book, such as the borrowed room, the rain through the roof, and Uncle Sarath’s house.

**10. Meet Magawa.** Research APOPO’s HeroRATs. How are the rats trained? Why don’t they set off the mines? Present what you find in a short talk or comic strip.

## **10. For Teachers and Parents**

**Reading pace.** The four parts make natural stopping points. One part a week, with the questions for that part, works well for a class. The Prologue belongs with Part One and the Epilogue with Part Four. Chapters are short enough to read aloud in one sitting.

**What to be aware of.** The book deals with disability, poverty, the death of a parent, and the danger of mines. An explosion happens offstage in Chapter 6 and an older neighbor is badly hurt. Maly’s accident is told briefly by Mae in Chapter 4. Nothing is graphic, but children who have lost a parent, who live with a disability, or who come from a region affected by war may connect with these scenes strongly. Let each child choose whether to speak or remain quiet; do not ask for personal testimony.

**Framing disability.** Maly survived an accident, but her character is not defined only by it. She has humor, wishes, anger, and the right to feel tired. Invite students to notice these different sides of her. When you compare lives, ask “What’s the same about your life and Maly’s?” before you ask “What’s different?”, and never “What does she lack?”

**The central lesson.** Sokha’s biggest mistake isn’t laziness or cruelty. It’s help given without asking. Students often side with Sokha at first, because his plan seems generous. Let them. Chapter 10 offers a chance to revisit and question that first reaction.

**Safety.** If you teach the safety rule, keep it calm and practical. The aim is a habit, not fear.

## **11. Reading Across the Series**

*My Corner of the World* follows four fictional young narrators in four countries on three continents. Each book can be read on its own, and they speak to each other in surprising ways.

| Book | Narrator | Where and when | The question at its heart |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| *Sokha’s Diary: The Road to the Bell* | Sokha, 12 | Banteay Meanchey, Cambodia, spring 2025 | How do you help without taking over? |
| *The Drum and the Ball: Lamine’s Journal* | Lamine, 12 | Bambali, Senegal, early 2024 | Can you follow your dream and still honor where you come from? |
| *Ananya’s Notebook: The Stolen Flame* | Ananya, about 12 | Jaipur, India, winter 2024 to 2025 | Is a win worth anything if it’s built on a lie? |
| *Anastasiya’s Echo: Katya’s Notebook* | Katya, 14 | Northeast Ukraine, 2024 to 2025 | How do you care for someone without disappearing? (For ages 13 and up) |

**Questions across the books**

1. All four narrators write in a notebook. Who is allowed to read it? Compare Sokha’s promise not to read Maly’s notebook without her permission with Baba reading Lamine’s journal without asking.

2. Sokha tries to protect his sister; Katya becomes responsible for hers. What do they both learn about the difference between protecting someone and deciding for them?

3. A bell rings at the end of Sokha’s story and at the end of Katya’s. What does each bell mean?
