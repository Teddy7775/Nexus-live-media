## **1. The Story in Brief**

Lamine NDiop is twelve, and he knows exactly what he wants to be: a professional soccer player, like Sadio Mané, who grew up in the very same village. His father, Baba, has a different plan in mind. Baba is a griot, a keeper of family memory, and Lamine is supposed to learn the names, the stories, and the drum that go with them.

On the first page of his journal, the team’s patched ball splits open for the third time. Soon everything else starts splitting too. An old friend, Cheikh, comes home from a soccer academy carrying a pair of shiny white cleats and calls their ball garbage. Lamine answers with a challenge: a real match, less than three weeks away, his team against Cheikh’s. To get ready, he starts cutting corners. He’s late for errands, tells lies to cover his practice time, and pushes his friends around on the field. The cost of his dream lands on his mother, his sister, his best friend, and his father.

*The Drum and the Ball* is a story about two rhythms, the beat of Baba’s drum and the bounce of a soccer ball, and about a boy who has to learn to listen before he can find his place in either one.

## **2. Where the Story Happens**

**Senegal**

Senegal is on the Atlantic coast of West Africa. The 2023 census counted about eighteen million people. The capital, Dakar, sits on a peninsula that reaches farther west than any other point on the African mainland. Senegal became independent from France in 1960.

French is the official language and the main language of instruction, which is why Lamine calls his teachers Madame and Monsieur. But most people speak one or more national languages at home. Wolof is the most widely spoken; others include Pulaar, Serer, Jola, Mandinka, and Soninke. Most Senegalese people are Muslim, and the country is known for its *teranga*, a Wolof word for hospitality. The national soccer team is nicknamed the Lions of Teranga.

**The Casamance**

The Gambia, a small country shaped like a river, cuts deep into Senegal. South of it lies the Casamance, the greenest part of the country, with rivers, rice fields, mangroves, forests, and huge kapok trees. It is divided into three regions: Ziguinchor, Sédhiou, and Kolda. The Casamance also went through a long separatist conflict that began in the 1980s. The novel does not address this conflict or attempt to summarize the region’s history.

**Bambali**

Bambali is a real village on the Casamance River, in the Sédhiou region. It is famous across Africa as the hometown of Sadio Mané, one of the best soccer players Senegal has ever produced. Mané has paid for a hospital and school facilities in Bambali, as well as the municipal stadium’s new artificial turf, inaugurated in January 2024, just weeks before Lamine’s journal begins.

**Getting there.** One road route from Dakar to the Casamance crosses the Gambia using the Senegambia Bridge, inaugurated in 2019. Distance and travel time depend on the route. Malang returns from Dakar for the wedding, but the novel does not say which route he takes.

**Other places in the book.** Lamine’s mother buys supplies in **Bounkiling**, a market town in the same region. **Ziguinchor**, the biggest city in the Casamance, is where a new customer comes from in Chapter 8.

## **3. Griots: The Keepers of Memory**

**What a Griot Does**

In many West African societies, including in Senegal, Mali, the Gambia, and Guinea, griots are the keepers of family memory. They know family trees going back many generations. They tell history, praise families and their ancestors, give advice, and bring ceremonies to life with words and music. In the book, Baba speaks at naming ceremonies, weddings, and big celebrations.

Griots are called by different names in different languages: *jali* in Mandinka, *jeli* in Bambara and Maninka, *gewel* in Wolof. Their knowledge is usually handed down in families, from parent to child. Depending on the tradition, they may accompany their words with the kora, a harp-lute usually fitted with 21 strings, the balafon, a wooden xylophone, or drums. Many of Senegal’s great singers, such as Youssou N’Dour, come from griot families.

**Oral History**

Griots are the reason some of West Africa’s great stories have survived for centuries. The most famous is the epic of Sundiata Keita, who founded the Mali Empire in the 1200s. It was passed down by generations of griots before anyone wrote it down.

The book shows what that kind of memory looks like up close. At the Faty family’s ceremony in Chapter 3, Baba recites a genealogy from memory: “Bakary, son of Moussa, whose canoe came home even when the sky changed its mind…” Each name carries a story. Forgetting one is like dropping a stitch in a long piece of cloth.

**Memory and Mistakes**

Baba also writes things down. He keeps notebooks of family genealogies, and he gets nervous when he loses his place. The book is honest about this: even the keepers of memory forget. When Lamine forgets his own ancestor’s name in Chapter 1, and when Baba loses his rhythm at the ceremony, it isn’t a failure of tradition. It’s what happens to anyone who carries a lot. What matters is who helps you find the road back.

## **4. Soccer in Senegal**

**A Soccer Nation**

Soccer is everywhere in Senegal: in city streets, on beaches, and on dusty village fields like the one behind Lamine’s school. Children play with whatever ball they can find, and teams like Lamine’s patch the same ball again and again.

| Year | Moment |
| :---- | :---- |
| 2002 | In its first World Cup, Senegal beats France, the defending champion, in the opening match and reaches the quarterfinals. |
| 2019 | Senegal reaches the final of the Africa Cup of Nations. |
| February 2022 | Senegal wins its first Africa Cup of Nations (the 2021 edition), beating Egypt on penalties. Sadio Mané scores the winning kick. |
| January 29, 2024 | Senegal is knocked out of the 2023 Africa Cup of Nations by the host country, Côte d’Ivoire, on penalties. It happens one week before Lamine’s journal begins. |

**Timeline note.** The sports information in this guide is anchored to the journal’s setting in February and March 2024. Later results and events can be explored through a separate, dated research activity with sources.

**Sadio Mané**

| Born | April 10, 1992, in Bambali |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Training** | Génération Foot, a soccer academy near Dakar |
| **Clubs** | Metz in France, Red Bull Salzburg in Austria, Southampton and Liverpool in England, Bayern Munich in Germany, then Al Nassr in Saudi Arabia, which he joined in 2023 |
| **Biggest wins** | The UEFA Champions League with Liverpool (2019) and the Africa Cup of Nations with Senegal (2022) |
| **Awards** | African Player of the Year in 2019 and 2022 |
| **National team** | Africa Cup of Nations winner with Senegal in February 2022, before the journal begins. |
| **Back home** | He has funded health, school, and soccer facilities in Bambali. |

Mané is proof that a boy from Bambali can reach the top. The book is careful about what that proof means. Baba asks Lamine, “How many make it?” When Lamine says he doesn’t know, Baba answers honestly: “Neither do I.”

**Academies and the Odds**

Every year, soccer academies in Senegal take in promising young players. They offer organized training, coaching, and sometimes a path to clubs abroad. Some of the best known are Génération Foot, where Mané trained, and Diambars, founded in 2003 by a group that included the former French star Patrick Vieira. Very few academy players become professionals, which is why many academies require their players to stay in school.

Cheikh’s story in the book shows the pressure behind the white cleats. As the book’s glossary says, getting into an academy “doesn’t guarantee a professional career.”

## **5. Daily Life in Bambali**

**A Day in Lamine’s Life**

These are Lamine’s activities in the novel. They describe this fictional family, not every family in Bambali or Senegal:

* **Early morning:** fetching water from the water point with basins and jugs before school, with his sister Awa.

* **Morning and early afternoon:** school, where lessons are in French.

* **Afternoon:** errands for his mother’s business, and chores at home.

* **Late afternoon:** soccer practice at five, until they have to quit before dark.

* **Evening:** family, homework, and Baba’s lessons, which later become “thirty minutes of storytelling on Mondays and Wednesdays.”

**Work and Money**

Lamine’s mother is a trader. She buys onions, peanuts, and tomatoes from a supplier in Bounkiling, sells them at the market, and records every sale in her account book. When a trip is missed or a customer is lost, the whole family feels it.

Senegal uses the West African CFA franc, which it shares with other countries in West Africa. Its value is tied to the euro. At the fixed euro exchange rate, 1,000 CFA francs are worth about €1.52. The value in US or Canadian dollars varies with exchange rates.

**Faith and Community**

Lamine’s fictional family is Muslim, as is much of Senegal’s population. The call to prayer from the mosque marks the day, and Friday prayers bring the community together in the early afternoon. The book’s Epilogue takes place during Ramadan, a month of fasting from dawn to sunset, prayer, sharing, and community. In 2024, Ramadan began in Senegal on March 11 or 12, depending on the community. How people take part depends on age, health, and family. In Chapter 13, just before Ramadan, Lamine’s little sister Awa announces that she’ll fast “at least until breakfast.” Ramadan ends with a big celebration that Senegalese people call Korité.

**Family**

Lamine lives with his parents, his sister, and his grandmother, Yaye Khady. Aunts, uncles, cousins, and neighbors come and go. Older women are called Auntie as a sign of respect. Family is also history: in Baba’s genealogies, every child belongs to a long line of names.

## **7. Discussion Questions**

**Understanding the Story**

**Part One: The Patched Ball (Chapters 1 to 4)**

1. Madame Diouf tells the class, “Write what keeps you up at night. Afterward, you’ll choose what you share.” What keeps Lamine up at night on the first day of his journal?

2. Lamine hides the split ball in his school bag and runs his errand late. What does that small choice cost his mother?

3. Mom tells Lamine, “A dream takes time, Lamine. If you’re not careful, yours will take other people’s time too.” What does she mean?

4. What happens at the Faty family’s ceremony in Chapter 3? How does Lamine help his father, and what does Baba tell him on the way home?

5. Why is Moussa so hurt in Chapter 4? When he brings the red laces, he says they don’t fix everything. What do they fix?

**Part Two: The Shadow of a Homecoming (Chapters 5 to 8)**

6. Lamine promises Moussa, “Even if he plays better, that doesn’t mean we’re worth nothing.” Does he keep that promise?

7. What do the white cleats say about Cheikh? What small clues suggest his life at the academy may be harder than it looks?

8. Who makes the challenge in Chapter 7? Baba says, “You gave your word on behalf of a whole team.” Why is that a problem?

9. What happens at 3:47 and at 4:03 in Chapter 8? What does Mom lose because of Lamine’s lie?

**Part Three: Learning to Listen (Chapters 9 to 13)**

10. Why does the team fall apart at practice in Chapter 9? Fatou asks, “Did you want a team or proof that you’re the best?” How would you answer for Lamine?

11. What do Lamine and Baba watch at the river in Chapter 10? What does the kingfisher teach Lamine?

12. What happens when Baba reads Lamine’s journal? Yaye Khady says, “A door is still a door, even inside your own house.” What does she mean?

13. When the village cleans up the field, Cheikh points out that the hole helps the home team, Souleymane agrees, and Lamine is tempted too. Why does Lamine finally say, “We fill the hole”?

14. What three things does Lamine promise the night before the match? Which one does he find the hardest?

**Part Four and Epilogue: Two Rhythms (Chapters 14 to 17 and the Epilogue)**

15. How is each goal scored? Why is the pass in Chapter 15 so important to the story?

16. After the match, Baba doesn’t make a speech. He puts his hand on Lamine’s shoulder and says, “You heard.” What did Lamine hear?

17. In the Fatys’ courtyard, Cheikh hands over the ball and admits something. What is it, and how does it change the way you see him?

18. Lamine reads Baba a page from his journal. Baba says, “I didn’t know you had seen my fear.” Which page does he choose, and why do you think he chose that one?

19. In the Epilogue, what do we learn about the ancestor Abdoulaye? What is the “third choice” Lamine discovers?

**Thinking Deeper**

1. At the start, Lamine thinks he has to choose between the drum and the ball. At the end he writes, “The two rhythms weren’t the same. They didn’t need to be.” What changed his mind?

2. Who pays for Lamine’s dream? Is it fair to ask your family to share the cost of your dream? Where is the line?

3. Baba read Lamine’s journal without permission, because he was worried. Was he wrong even though he meant well? What makes his apology work?

4. There are several apologies in the book: Lamine’s to Awa, Lamine’s to Moussa on the night of the red laces, Baba’s apology without a “but,” and Cheikh’s apology for calling the ball garbage. What makes an apology real?

5. Cheikh seems to have everything Lamine wants. What is he carrying that nobody sees?

6. Lamine admits he’s afraid of becoming “somebody ordinary.” Is being ordinary something to fear?

7. Even griots forget. Why does it matter that the book shows Baba losing his place, and an ancestor who forgot things too?

8. Listening shows up on the field, in music, and in the family. How is listening on a soccer field like listening to a drum?

## **8. Activities**

**1. What keeps you up at night.** Follow Madame Diouf’s instruction. Write a journal page about something on your mind. Then read it again and mark only the lines you would share. You decide; nobody else does.

**2. A name, a story.** Baba’s genealogy follows a pattern: “Bakary, son of Moussa, whose canoe came home even when the sky changed its mind.” Write three lines in that shape about people who matter to you: a name, who they come from, and one thing they did that deserves to be remembered. Your lines can be about a relative, a friend, a teacher, or a neighbor. Read them aloud with a steady beat.

**3. Two rhythms.** Clap Baba’s drum pattern, “Doum. Doum-doum. Pa.” Then tap the ball pattern, “Poc. Poc-poc.” In small groups, put them together into one rhythm, the way Lamine and Baba do in the Epilogue: “Poc. Doum. Poc.” Perform it for the class.

**4. Match report.** Write the big match as a radio commentator or a newspaper reporter. Use the exact minutes from the book: the first goal in the 9th minute, the three passes in the 16th, the Red Pass in the 23rd, the block in the 41st, and the winner in the 47th.

**5. Fatou’s statistics.** At recess or in gym class, keep statistics on a game the way Fatou does: passes, lost balls, shots. What do the numbers show that nobody noticed while playing?

**6. The kingfisher.** Watch a bird or another animal for ten minutes, outside or in a video. Write down every time it waits, tries, and tries again. What does patience look like?

**7. What a week costs.** Chapter 8 is called “What a Week Costs.” Make a chart of everything Lamine’s secret practice on Thursday cost: in time, in money, and in trust. Then make a chart for something you want badly. Who else would pay part of the price?

**8. Make a field fair.** In Chapter 12, the village cleans up the field together. With an adult, plan a cleanup of a shared space at your school or in your neighborhood. Leave sharp objects and hazardous repairs to adults. Who would you invite? What would you fix first?

**9. Debate.** “Parents should be allowed to read their children’s journals.” Use Chapter 11 to support both sides.

**10. Map the journey.** On a map of Senegal, trace one possible road route from Dakar to Bambali across the Gambia. Find the Casamance River, Ziguinchor, and Sédhiou. Estimate the length of that route, then find a place a similar distance from your home. How long would it take to get there?

## **9. Going Further**

**Books for Young Readers**

* ***Booked*** by Kwame Alexander. A novel in verse about Nick, twelve, who loves soccer and is facing big changes at home. For ages 10 to 12.

* ***Sundiata: A Legend of Africa*** by Will Eisner. A graphic novel that retells the epic of the founder of the Mali Empire, the very kind of story griots have kept alive for centuries.

* ***Akissi: Tales of Mischief*** by Marguerite Abouet, illustrated by Mathieu Sapin. Funny comics about a girl growing up in Côte d’Ivoire. For ages 7 to 11.

**Listen**

* The kora music of **Toumani Diabaté**, a Malian musician from a long line of griots.

* The songs of **Youssou N’Dour**, one of Senegal’s most famous singers, who comes from a griot family on his mother’s side.

## **10. For Teachers and Parents**

**Reading pace.** The four parts make natural stopping points. One part a week, with the questions for that part, works well. Part Four is short and fast, so you can read it together with the Epilogue.

**What to be aware of.** The book contains no graphic violence. It includes verbal humiliation, arguments, and physical contact during sports. The hard moments are about lies, broken promises, family arguments, and the pressure to succeed. Students who feel pushed toward one path by their families, or who feel torn between two cultures, may connect strongly with Lamine and with Cheikh.

**Respecting the details.** The book doesn’t name Lamine’s ethnic group or home language, and it presents Ramadan and daily prayer simply, as part of village life. Follow its lead. Avoid presenting Senegal or the Casamance as a place of hardship to be pitied. Bambali in the book has a stadium with new turf, a busy market, school, soccer, music, and a family that argues and makes up like families everywhere.

**About Sadio Mané.** Section 4 gives background for the journal’s setting in 2024. If students explore later sports news, distinguish it from the story’s period and record the date and source of the information.

**The central lesson.** Lamine doesn’t have to give up soccer or tradition. He has to stop playing alone. The book’s best conversations come from Chapter 9, when the team falls apart, and from Chapter 11, when Baba reads the journal. Give those chapters extra time.

## **11. Reading Across the Series**

*My Corner of the World* follows four fictional young narrators in four countries on three continents. Each book can be read on its own, and they speak to each other in surprising ways.

| Book | Narrator | Where and when | The question at its heart |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| *Sokha’s Diary: The Road to the Bell* | Sokha, 12 | Banteay Meanchey, Cambodia, spring 2025 | How do you help without taking over? |
| *The Drum and the Ball: Lamine’s Journal* | Lamine, 12 | Bambali, Senegal, early 2024 | Can you follow your dream and still honor where you come from? |
| *Ananya’s Notebook: The Stolen Flame* | Ananya, about 12 | Jaipur, India, winter 2024 to 2025 | Is a win worth anything if it’s built on a lie? |
| *Anastasiya’s Echo: Katya’s Notebook* | Katya, 14 | Northeast Ukraine, 2024 to 2025 | How do you care for someone without disappearing? (For ages 13 and up) |

**Questions across the books**

1. Lamine and Sokha are both twelve, and both are sure they know best. What does each of them have to learn to do before he can really help?

2. Lamine’s father reads his journal without asking. Sokha promises not to read his sister’s notebook without her permission. Ananya’s teacher reads only the pages marked with a star. What should the rules be?

3. Ananya and Lamine both lie to make time for their dream, and someone else pays. Compare what Lamine’s mother loses with what Ananya’s father nearly loses.
