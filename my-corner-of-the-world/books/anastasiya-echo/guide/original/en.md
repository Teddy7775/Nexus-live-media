# **1. The Story in Brief**

On the first day of school, Katya irons her white blouse and walks to the gate, even though nobody knows if the school will open. At eight o’clock, the bell doesn’t ring. She records a minute of that silence on her phone and starts a red notebook addressed to the three men of her family, who are all away: Papa, who repairs railroad lines, and her older brothers, Maksym and Bohdan.

At home, her mother is pregnant and more tired than she admits. On November 28, a strike destroys the bridge and knocks out the power while Mama is in early labor, which she has hidden since morning. By morning Katya has a baby sister and has lost her mother. Among Mama’s last words are three short sentences Katya will carry all year: “You’re her sister.” “Not her mother.” “A sister can love without disappearing.”

The rest of the year tests every one of those words. Katya, her sister Anastasiya, their neighbor Baba Vira, and Vira’s grandson Ihor are evacuated to Lypove, a village where seven families live in the cellar of an old school. There is cold, hunger, a frightening fever, a school that meets underground, a festival of sounds, and a night when the shelter itself is hit. And then a journalist arrives, and Katya has to decide what the world is allowed to hear.

*Anastasiya’s Echo* is a novel about grief and responsibility, about accepting help, and about the right of every person, even in the middle of a war, to decide how their own story is told.

## **How the Book Is Built**

Every chapter opens with a sound: one that is lost, kept, broken, or found again. Together, those sounds tell the story of the year.

# **2. Where the Story Happens**

## **Ukraine**

Ukraine is the largest country entirely in Europe. Its capital is Kyiv. Before 2022, more than forty million people lived there. Ukraine has wide farmland, forests, the Carpathian Mountains in the west, and a coast on the Black Sea in the south. Ukrainian is the state language. Many Ukrainians also speak Russian, especially in the east and south, and many speak both.

## **The Northeast**

Katya’s story takes place in northeast Ukraine, in the part of the country that borders Russia. The real regions here include **Sumy** and **Kharkiv**. Kharkiv, Ukraine’s second largest city, is a major center for universities and industry. Since the full-scale invasion in 2022, cities and villages in this border area have lived with regular shelling, missile and drone strikes, and long power outages.

## **Invented Places**

Katya’s hometown is never named in the book. It is a city with trams, a bridge, apartment buildings, and a market, and it is made up. **Lypove**, the village where the family goes in December, is invented too. Its name comes from *lypa*, the Ukrainian word for the linden tree. In the Epilogue, the family lives in an old boarding school turned into housing for displaced families, somewhere in the west of the country.

When you teach the book, keep these places unnamed and invented, as the author did. That way, the story belongs to many towns at once and doesn’t point to any real family.

# **3. Ukraine and the War: Background**

## **A Short Timeline**

| When | What happened |
| :---- | :---- |
| August 24, 1991 | Ukraine declares independence as the Soviet Union breaks apart. Ukrainians confirm it in a referendum that December. |
| Winter 2013 to 2014 | Mass protests in Kyiv, known as the Revolution of Dignity. |
| 2014 | Russia annexes Crimea, and war begins in the eastern region of Donbas. |
| February 24, 2022 | Russia launches a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Millions of people flee their homes. |
| 2024 | Fighting and strikes intensify along the northeast border. In May, the first school built entirely underground opens in Kharkiv. On October 1, the authorities announce a decision made the day before: the mandatory evacuation of families with children from 90 towns and villages in the Sumy district. |
| 2024 to 2025 | The school year of Katya’s notebook. Strikes on power plants bring long blackouts, especially in winter. |

The book’s own Note on the Story says it plainly: “Since February 24, 2022, Russia has been waging a full-scale invasion of Ukraine.” Inside the story, though, Katya never names who fires the missiles. She writes about what the war does to people: to her family, to her neighbors, and to children who want to be late for school for ordinary reasons. That choice keeps the reader’s attention on civilians.

## **Life After Leaving Home**

Since 2022, millions of Ukrainians have had to leave home, some to other parts of Ukraine and some to other countries. Families like Katya’s often live for months in temporary housing such as dormitories, schools, and reception centers, while they wait for news of relatives and try to rebuild something like normal life.

When someone is missing, families search through official lists, hotlines, and humanitarian organizations. The International Committee of the Red Cross runs a special tracing office to help families find news of people missing in this war. At the end of the book, Papa’s name is still “on the search lists.”

# **4. Daily Life in Wartime**

## **Air Raid Alerts and Shelters**

An **air raid alert** warns that missiles or drones may be coming. In Ukraine, alerts come through sirens, the radio, and phone apps, sometimes several times a day or in the middle of the night. When an alert sounds, people go to a designated **shelter**, following local instructions. Some are equipped basements or other underground spaces. In Lypove, the cellar of the old school is a shelter, a dormitory, and a classroom all at once.

## **Blackouts**

Strikes on power plants and power lines can leave whole cities without electricity, heat, or water for hours or days. Families charge phones and power banks whenever they can, cook when the power is on, and plan their days around outage schedules. Since the winter of 2022, cities have set up **Points of Invincibility**, heated spaces where anyone can get warm, charge a phone, get water, and connect to the internet. In Chapter 4, the power in Katya’s apartment dies at 4:12 in the afternoon, and the last electric sound she hears is the hum of the refrigerator.

## **Phones and Messages**

For families spread across the country, phones are a lifeline. Voice notes let Papa, Maksym, and Bohdan speak to Katya even when they can’t call. But messages depend on **relays**, the antennas that carry cell phone signals. When relays lose power or are damaged, whole areas go silent for days. Chapter 16, “No More Messages,” is about that silence.

## **School**

In Ukraine, the school year traditionally begins on **September 1**, the Day of Knowledge, with a ceremony called the **first bell**. An older student often carries a first grader on one shoulder while she rings a little bell. In 2024, September 1 fell on a Sunday, so classes began on Monday, September 2. Students are graded on a scale of 1 to 12, which is why Katya mentions a math grade that dropped “below ten out of twelve.”

Schools can only welcome students in person if they have a safe shelter. Near the front line, many students learn online. Others take classes in basements, in subway stations, and, in Kharkiv, in schools built underground. The cellar school in Lypove, where Olena Petrivna teaches two hours a day, is a small version of something very real.

## **Evacuation and Volunteers**

When an area becomes too dangerous, the authorities can order people to leave. Families with children are often the first ones required to go. Volunteers and public services organize buses, trucks, trains, and places to stay. In Chapter 7, the rule for the truck is “One person, one bag.”

## **Names**

Ukrainians often have three names: a first name, a patronymic formed from the father’s first name, and a family name. Olena Petrivna means “Olena, daughter of Petro.” Adults such as teachers are addressed politely by their first name and patronymic. Nearly every first name also has several loving short forms: Kateryna becomes Katya, and Anastasiya becomes Nastia.

## **Easter**

Easter is one of the most important holidays in Ukraine. Families bake **paska**, a rich, sweet bread, and take it to church to be blessed. People greet each other with “Christ is risen!” and answer “He is risen indeed!” In 2025, Orthodox and Catholic Christians celebrated Easter on the same day, April 20. In Chapter 16, the families in the cellar share a paska in the middle of a long silence.

# **6. Sound, Memory, and Consent**

## **Why Sound?**

Since the spring before the story begins, Katya has been recording her neighborhood for a school radio project called *The Sounds of My Neighborhood*: the baker knocking her paddle against the oven, the squeal of the tram, the dog at number twelve. During the war, she keeps recording. A recording can’t fix anything, but it can prove that a moment was real. As she writes after recording her sister’s laugh in Chapter 15, “This file doesn’t prove we’re okay. It proves this moment happened.”

Sound also lets the book talk about war without showing it. We hear the silence where a school bell should be, the knocks of a brother who can’t speak freely, and a baby’s laugh. The pictures of the ruins, as Katya says about the news report, “came later.”

## **Who Owns a Story?**

Katya’s biggest discovery isn’t about sound. It’s about permission. Over the year, she builds her own set of rules for recording and writing about other people.

* **In Chapter 13**, she stops asking, “Can I keep your sentence in my notebook?” and starts asking, “Do you want me to keep it, give it back to you, or copy it without your name?”

* **In Chapter 18**, every person in the Register of the Living chooses how they appear: full name, first name only, or no name; a sentence they dictate, one in their own handwriting, or a drawing. They can come back later to correct it, cross it out, or take their page back.

* **In Chapter 20**, she tells Daryna, “You can take part without being recorded,” and writes down Daryna’s rule: “You can take the sound. Not my name.”

Ihor sums it up in Chapter 19: “Speaking and being exposed aren’t the same thing.”

## **A Journalist Who Asks First**

When the journalist Aïcha Mbala arrives with the convoy, the first thing Katya says is “Help Vira first.” Aïcha switches off the camera in front of her. Later, in a tent with no camera and no recorder, she asks: “What do you want to protect?” Together they agree on conditions, written in both languages:

* Anastasiya’s name may be used, but not her face.

* Katya sees the footage and checks the translation of her words, and the images they agreed to protect are taken out.

* No exact location of the shelter, the evacuation road, or the relays.

* Katya is Anastasiya’s “sister,” never her “mother.”

* No one’s page is filmed without their consent and, when needed, the consent of their responsible adult. If the adult says yes but the child says no, the answer is no.

* Katya can say stop at any time, “even after saying yes.”

Aïcha is also honest about what she can’t promise. Translation and editing will shorten things, clips may be shared again, and she can’t control what strangers write in the comments. “That’s why you have to know before you say yes.”

## **Real Rules for Real Reporting**

Aïcha follows several principles of careful reporting on children. Consent alone does not guarantee protection: a voice or a story can identify someone even without a face or exact location. If the risk remains, the story should not be published. UNICEF’s principles for ethical reporting on children say that a child’s dignity and rights come first; that journalists should get consent from the child and a responsible adult; that they should protect the privacy of any child who could be put at risk, for example by changing names or hiding faces and locations; and that no story is worth harming a child. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, which almost every country in the world has ratified, protects a child’s right to be heard (Article 12), to express themselves (Article 13), to privacy (Article 16), to education (Article 28), and to protection and care in times of war (Article 38).

Katya’s letter puts the same idea in her own words: “A destroyed house doesn’t destroy our right to say no.”

# **7. Discussion Questions**

## **Understanding the Story**

**Part One: The Silent Bell (Chapters 1 to 6)**

1. Why does Katya iron her uniform and go to the school gate when nobody knows if school will open? What does she record at eight o’clock?

2. Katya writes to Papa, Maksym, and Bohdan in her red notebook, and adds, “I didn’t write *if*.” Why does that matter to her?

3. In Chapter 3, Katya writes, “My lie looked like kindness.” What is she hiding in her replies, and why?

4. On November 28, why can’t help reach the apartment?

5. Among Mama’s last words are “You’re her sister.” “Not her mother.” “A sister can love without disappearing.” Her very last are “Find the others… Don’t carry it all…” What was Mama trying to protect Katya from?

6. How does Katya choose her sister’s name? What does the note “*We rise again*” mean to her?

**Part Two: Breath in Winter (Chapters 7 to 12)**

7. The rule is “One person, one bag.” What does Katya leave behind, and what comes with her?

8. On the walk through the snow, Katya lets Vira carry the baby. Why does “relief” feel “like betrayal”?

9. What is on Katya’s “List of Forbidden Feelings,” and what does Vira add to it? What does Vira mean by “*Actions count. Feelings pass. Go to sleep*”?

10. Why does Katya hide her sister’s fever? Vira asks her, “Your sister or your secret?” What does Katya finally choose?

11. The families in the cellar have to choose between heating fuel and a trip to the clinic. How do they decide? What does the nurse mean when she says, “A list of thanks isn’t a bill”?

**Part Three: Lost Voices (Chapters 13 to 18)**

12. How does Katya change the way she asks other children for their sounds in Chapter 13?

13. In the barn loft, Ihor tells Katya, “You don’t get to draw a circle around people and call it protecting them.” What is their fight about? Who do you think is right?

14. Katya first refuses to delete the recording, then asks Ihor again. What is the difference between keeping a file and playing it for someone else? What should she do if he still wanted it deleted?

15. What does Katya mean when she writes, “I didn’t stop waiting. I only stopped building a fence out of the days”?

16. What news does the red envelope bring? Why does Katya write a letter to God, tear it up, and then tape it back together?

17. Why does Katya refuse to sort people into columns for “dead,” “wounded,” and “missing”? What is the Register of the Living?

**Part Four: What the World Hears (Chapters 19 to 24 and the Epilogue)**

18. On the radio, Bohdan says there is nothing certain about Papa, and “I’m not going to make anything up.” Why does that honesty matter to Katya?

19. At the festival of sounds, how does Katya include Daryna, who doesn’t raise her hand to be recorded? What rule does Daryna set?

20. On the night of June 5, Katya has to choose between the notebook and Pavlo. What does she choose? What does she mean by “That night, I had chosen a living hand”?

21. When Aïcha asks if she wants to tell them what happened, what does Katya answer? What does Aïcha do?

22. In the tent, before any recording, Aïcha asks Katya, “What do you want to protect?” What does Katya answer? Which of their conditions seems most important to you? Is there one you would add?

23. Read the letter “To Those in Charge” in Chapter 24. Which line stays with you, and why?

24. In the Epilogue, what did the news report change, and what didn’t it change?

25. Why does Katya cry when Anastasiya says “Ka-ya”?

## **Thinking Deeper**

1. “A sister can love without disappearing.” Over the year, when does Katya almost disappear into caring for Anastasiya? What helps her find her way back?

2. The book is full of kind lies: Katya’s replies to her family, the hidden fever. It also has hard truths that help: Bohdan’s “Nothing certain,” the nurse’s note, “*she told the truth in the end*.” Is it ever right to hide the truth to protect someone?

3. Katya struggles to accept help: from Mariya, who offers to feed the baby, on the snowy road, and in the cellar. Why is accepting help so hard? What changes her mind?

4. Katya’s letter asks those in charge: “Don’t only count our dead. Count the notebooks left empty, the birthdays that got interrupted, the jobs we wanted to learn, and the bad jokes nobody recorded.” What do numbers leave out? Why do we still need numbers?

5. Aïcha tells Katya, “Part of my job is knowing what I don’t have the right to take.” What do journalists owe the people they film? What do readers and viewers owe them?

6. The report “had brought messages and a few crates. It hadn’t stopped the war.” Was it worth telling the story? What can a story do, and what can’t it do?

7. Katya is angry at God, and the book lets her be angry. Vira answers, “I’m not his lawyer.” How do different characters in the book deal with faith, doubt, and hope?

8. Inside the story, Katya never names who is attacking. Why might the author have made that choice? What does it make you notice?

9. Katya counts steps, breaths, and supplies when she’s scared, and slowly learns to listen instead. How do counting and listening show two different ways of dealing with fear?

10. The last line of the book is “This time, the echo didn’t come from the guns.” What is the “echo” in the title?

# **8. Activities**

Work may remain private. Recording a voice or creating a class register does not automatically permit publication: sharing requires separate permission, following school rules and the permissions needed for minors. Also decide who keeps the files and when they will be deleted.

These activities never ask students to share painful personal experiences. Every one of them can be done with invented or chosen material, and, following Katya’s own rule, anyone can take part without being recorded.

**1. A sound map.** In Chapter 2, Katya writes a “Sound Map of the Apartment.” Make a sound map of a place you know well, or of a place you invent: a home, a street, a school. Label each sound with where it comes from and when you hear it. Which sound makes the place feel most like itself?

**2. Seven seconds.** Katya keeps seven seconds of her sister’s laugh. Choose a moment seven seconds long that you’d like to keep, from any day, and describe it in writing so precisely that someone else could almost hear it. You can also record it, with permission from anyone whose voice is in it.

**3. A class consent charter.** Before any interview, podcast, or photo project, write your class’s version of Katya’s box “What Can Leave the Notebook.” Decide together what always needs permission, what never leaves the room, and how anyone can say stop, “even after saying yes.”

**4. A Register of the Living.** Create a class register of people who make your school or neighborhood work: the bus driver, the cook, a coach, a neighbor. Ask each person how they’d like to appear: full name, first name, or no name; a sentence, a quote, or a drawing. Respect every answer, including no.

**5. The ethics editor.** In small groups, act as editors at a news outlet. A reporter has sent a story about children in a shelter, with photos, names, and a location. Using Section 6 and UNICEF’s principles, decide what can be published, what must change, and what must be cut. Explain every decision.

**6. To those in charge.** Write your own letter to someone with real power to decide, such as a mayor, a school board, or a lawmaker, about something children in your community need. Follow the shape of Katya’s letter: what you don’t want to be called, what you want, what you need, and what you want them to count.

**7. Two timelines.** Make a timeline of Katya’s year using the chapter dates. Beside it, add the real events mentioned in the book’s Note on the Story: the first day of school on September 2, 2024, the evacuation announcement on October 1, 2024, and Easter on April 20, 2025. How does fiction use real dates?

**8. Sound design.** Katya dreams of making “the sound for movies.” Choose a scene, such as the festival of sounds in Chapter 20, and recreate its soundscape with everyday objects: a pot, a book slammed shut, a bicycle bell, water. Perform it live while someone reads the scene aloud. Stop before the alert at the end of the chapter, and don’t recreate sirens, explosions, or strikes.

**9. Debate.** “Journalists should never film children in a war zone.” Or: “Katya should have deleted the recording when Ihor asked.” Compare the arguments using evidence from the book. Distinguish Katya’s first refusal from the limited permission Ihor later gives; no one has to defend a right to record against someone’s refusal.

**10. The Sounds That Come Back.** In the Epilogue, Katya starts a radio workshop where every student can bring a sound from their life or keep their silence. Run your own version as a class podcast, with your consent charter from Activity 3.

## **Reading with Care**

* **Preview the book.** Read it yourself first, especially Part One. Consider reading Chapters 4 to 6 together in class rather than assigning them alone.

* **Tell students what’s coming.** A short, calm warning before hard chapters helps readers feel in control. The book’s own Note to the Reader is a good model.

* **Never require personal stories.** Students may have lived through war, displacement, or the loss of a parent. Don’t ask them to share, and don’t treat a student from Ukraine, or from any country at war, as the class expert. Let them decide if and how they take part, the way Katya lets Daryna decide.

* **Offer quiet ways in.** Offer several options: writing, drawing, a small group, or an alternative activity. Students can choose without having to explain a refusal; a small group will not suit everyone.

* **Watch and follow up.** If a student seems distressed, check in privately and involve your school counselor. For families from Ukraine, the foundation Voices of Children offers psychological support, including online.

## **Getting the Details Right**

* Katya’s hometown is never named, and Lypove is invented. Don’t place the story in a real city.

* Papa repairs railroad lines, Maksym works in a field hospital, and Bohdan works in communications for civil defense. The book doesn’t call them soldiers.

* Mama is not killed by a bomb. She dies of a complication after giving birth, on a night when a strike has destroyed the bridge and no rescue team can get through.

* Katya is Anastasiya’s sister, never her mother. The book insists on that difference, and so does Katya in her interview.

* Vira is a neighbor, not Katya’s grandmother. Ihor is Katya’s friend.

* Papa’s fate and Mykhailo’s fate are not resolved. Resist the urge to resolve them for students.

## **Framing the War**

The book’s Note states the facts: since February 24, 2022, Russia has been waging a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Present that history accurately and calmly. Inside the story, the focus is on civilians, children’s rights, and the people who help. Keep your classroom’s focus there too, and avoid graphic news footage. If students raise current events, stick to reliable sources and remember that some students may have ties to Ukraine, to Russia, or to both. No student should be asked to speak for a country or a government.

## **Reading Pace**

The four parts make natural stopping points. Part One covers the most time and holds the hardest chapters; give it room. Parts Two and Three can move faster. Part Four covers only five days but is the longest part, so consider splitting it after Chapter 21. One part a week, with the questions for that part, works well.

# **11. Reading Across the Series**

*My Corner of the World* follows four young writers in four countries on three continents. The first three books are aimed mainly at independent readers ages 9 to 13; some readers may enjoy them with adult support from age 8. *Anastasiya’s Echo* is for readers 13 and up. Each book can be read on its own, and they speak to each other in surprising ways.

| Book | Narrator | Where and when | The question at its heart |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| *Sokha’s Diary: The Road to the Bell* | Sokha, 12 | Banteay Meanchey, Cambodia, spring 2025 | How do you help without taking over? |
| *The Drum and the Ball: Lamine’s Journal* | Lamine, 12 | Bambali, Senegal, early 2024 | Can you follow your dream and still honor where you come from? |
| *Ananya’s Notebook: The Stolen Flame* | Ananya, 12 | Jaipur, India, winter 2024 to 2025 | Is a win worth anything if it’s built on a lie? |
| *Anastasiya’s Echo: Katya’s Notebook* | Katya, 14 | Northeast Ukraine, 2024 to 2025 | How do you care for someone without disappearing? |

**Questions across the books**

1. Sokha learns to walk beside his sister: “Next to you. Not in front.” Katya learns that “a sister can love without disappearing.” What do these two older siblings learn about caring for a younger sister?

2. All four narrators keep a notebook. Sokha promises not to read his sister’s without her permission. Lamine’s father reads his without asking. Ananya’s teacher reads only the pages marked with a star. Katya lends hers to a journalist, with permission marked page by page and a signed receipt. Who should decide who reads your words?

3. A bell rings at the end of Sokha’s story and at the end of Katya’s. What does each bell mean?
